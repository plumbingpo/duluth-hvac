A modern furnace runs a strict startup sequence, and knowing where the sequence breaks is most of the diagnosis. The thermostat calls, the inducer motor spins up to prove draft through a pressure switch, the hot surface igniter glows, the gas valve opens, the burners light, the flame sensor confirms fire, and only then does the blower deliver heat to the house. In Duluth, MN, the failures cluster at predictable points: an oxide-coated flame sensor, a hot surface igniter cracked from years of thermal cycling, a pressure switch that will not close because the inducer is weak or the condensate trap on a high-efficiency model has clogged, or a control board with a burned relay. Each one stops the sequence cold, and each one mimics the others from the hallway.

Rather than guess, Duluth HVAC tests the sequence one stage at a time. We read igniter resistance, measure flame signal in microamps, check that the pressure switch responds to real inducer draft, confirm voltage at the gas valve, and test the blower and its capacitor. Because this is a fuel-burning appliance, the safety basics come with every visit: we inspect the heat exchanger, leak-test the gas connections, and verify flue draft. You cannot tell a furnace problem from a furnace hazard by looking at the thermostat. Before we leave, temperature rise is measured against the data plate so airflow and flame are matched as the manufacturer intended.

We work on gas, propane, and electric models from every major brand, both conventional 80 percent units and condensing high-efficiency models with their traps, drains, and dual pressure switches. The truck stock covers the parts that actually fail: igniters, flame sensors, pressure switches, capacitors, inducer assemblies, and blower motors, so most repairs finish in one trip. Warning Signs for Furnace Repair

Recognizing the warning signs early can save you time, money, and prevent significant damage to your property. These are the signals our technicians see most often when a problem is underway:

  • Frequent on-off cycling that never reaches the thermostat setting
  • Rust streaks, soot, or water around the cabinet or flue
  • A bang or boom when the burners ignite late
  • Squeals, scrapes, or grinding noises behind the blower door
  • The blower runs constantly but the air never warms
  • A flame that burns soft and yellow rather than steady blue
  • Repeated shutdowns just seconds after the burners light
  • The igniter glows and clicks, yet no flame ever appears

Why Hire a Professional for Furnace Repair?

A furnace combines gas, flame, high voltage, and a flue in one cabinet, and the failure that matters most is the one you cannot see from outside: a compromised heat exchanger passing combustion gases into the air your blower pushes through the house. Delayed ignition that booms at light-off stresses that exchanger further every morning it goes unfixed. Meanwhile the diagnostic traps stack up: a furnace that trips its high limit may have a bad switch, or it may be protecting itself from a clogged filter or a dying blower, and replacing the switch in the second case just removes a safety while leaving the fault.

Our techs look at every symptom in the context of the whole furnace. We measure flame signal instead of assuming it, which keeps us from replacing a healthy sensor while a cracked igniter or weak gas valve goes unnoticed. Draft is confirmed at the pressure switch and at the flue. Temperature rise is checked against the data plate, so airflow issues are caught before they cut years off the heat exchanger. The gas side is leak-tested with instruments, never with a quick sniff. What you get is a furnace proven safe over a full cycle, plus an honest opinion if the unit is getting close to the point where replacing it beats repairing it again.

Maintenance Tips for Furnace Repair

A little routine maintenance goes a long way toward avoiding a mid-season breakdown. Start with these practical measures our team relies on:

  • Give the furnace room to breathe, since a crowded utility space starves the burners of air
  • Put a carbon monoxide detector on every level and test them monthly
  • Notice how the furnace sounds at startup, because a quiet light-off is healthy and a boom is not
  • Replace the filter every month while the furnace is working hard, since it costs less than any other part
  • If you have a high-efficiency furnace, check that the condensate drain is flowing freely
  • Book a fall tune-up so weak igniters and fouled sensors are caught before the first freeze
  • Keep storage, lint, and especially chlorine-based products away from the furnace closet
